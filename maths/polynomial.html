Polynomials in mathematics refer to a general set of functions that meet the following criteria:

  • Must contain at least one term.
  • Must not have infinite terms
  • Criteria for a term: Must not contain the following: division of variables, or negative exponentials
  • Can contain multiple variables (x, y, z)
  • Does not require a variable

$$f(x)=x^3-4x^2$$

Polynomials have a number of key parts that can be found, just by looking at them. The degree of a polynomial is the largest exponent that is attached to a variable in the equation. An example of this is on the example equation on the right, where $x$ is to the power of 3. This is the largest exponent in the polynomial is 3 so this is the degree. While we are talking about exponentials and polynomials it might be useful to mention standard form. Standard form of a polynomial is quite simple, the terms are ordered highest exponential to lowest exponential. If two terms have the same exponent then simply order them by their coefficient (variables come before non-variables).

There are methods of finding the x-intercepts, the shape of the function, and any y-intercepts. This guide will go over them.

Note that the x-intercepts can only be found if the polynomial is in factored form or is a quadratic. This guide covers what factored form is but does not go over how to factor. For a guide on this, go here:

Polynomial guide

Factored form varies from equation to equation but looks something like this: $$(x+2)\cdot(x-2)$$ Solving for the x-intercepts in factored form is very simple. All you need to do is set each linear term equal to zero and solve for x.
This is shown on the right.

If an equation is in the standard quadratic form ($ax^2+bx-c$, or similar), the quadratic formula can be used to solve for the x-intercepts. The quadratic formula looks something like this:
$$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$$ If the equation is in standard quadratic form, $ax^2-bx-c$, where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants (not variables) simply plug them into the quadratic formula and solve. Example on the right.

Next up is the y-intercept. This one is super easy because a function can only cross $y=0$ once (or any y point for that matter). To solve for the y-intercept of any polynomial or function just set it equal to zero. Simple example:
$$f(x)=x+1$$ $$0=x+1$$ $$x=-1$$ The y-intercept here is -1.

Finally let's talk about function shape. A function's shape can be determined just by looking at it and by doing almost no math. Take the function:
$$(x+4)^2(x+1)$$
I know that this function will come up from $-\infty$ and bounce off the graph at 4, then strikethrough at 1. Finally it will ascend into the heavens and go up forever ($\lim x \to \infty$). Did I cheat and look at a graph? Nope!

The first thing that I did was find the x-intercepts. This is essential to know when finding the shape because you need to know where the line intercepts the x-intercept to make an accurate graph with labeled points. The next thing I did is look at the number of x's in the equation. There are three when all the exponents are expanded out ($(x+4)^2=(x+4)(x+4)$) and this meant that both lines that infinitely extend would extend in opposite directions (think of a linear function). I knew this because there were three and three is an odd number. When you have an odd number of x's in the equation the line must extend in opposite directions. When you have an even number of x's in the function both ends of the line must extend in the same direction. The way a function behaves when it extends infinitely is known as end behavior. Then I looked at what power each linear term was set to. The one that was $(x+4)^2$ was to an even power so I knew it would bounce off the x-intercept. The other linear term only had one x so I knew that it would strike through the x-intercept. And I know what x-intercept points the linear terms were tied to so I just corresponded those with the fact of whether they bounced or not.

$$example$$ $$f(x)=x^3-4x^2$$

$$factoring\ example$$ $$(x+2)\cdot(x-2)$$ $$x+2=0$$ $$x=-2$$ $$x-2=0$$ $$x=2$$ $$The\ x\ intercepts\ are\ (2,0)\ and\ (-2,0)$$

$$Quadratic\ Example$$ $$x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$$ $$a=1$$ $$b=-5$$ $$c=6$$ $$Plug\ In$$ $$x=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{-5^2-4\cdot 1 \cdot 6}}{2\cdot1}$$ $$x=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{25-24}}{2}$$ $$x=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{1}}{2}$$ $$x=\frac{5\pm1}{2}$$ $$Solve\ for\ both\ +\ and\ -$$ $$x=\frac{5+1}{2}$$ $$x=\frac{6}{2}$$ $$x=3$$ $$x=\frac{5-1}{2}$$ $$x=\frac{4}{2}$$ $$x=2$$ $$The\ X\ intercepts\ are\ 3\ and\ 2$$

Practice Problem/Real World Example: The insanity roller coaster

There is a roller coaster at a theme park. The coaster's height over time can be modeled with the following function: $$f(x)=(x+2)^2(x-2)^2(x-8)^8$$ Find... When the roller coaster is at zero feet.
How many times the roller coaster is at zero feet.
Does the roller coaster ever go underground?
Where the roller coaster is coming from and where it is going (end behavior).

$Answer$ The roller coaster touches the ground three times. The roller coaster touches the ground at 2, -2, and 8. The roller coaster never goes underground. The roller coaster comes from $\infty$ and goes to $\infty$

Practice Problem/Real World Example: Insanity roller coaster 2

A roller coaster starts underground and moves upwards. One second into the ride you reach the surface and climb up. You then dive back underground and go from above ground to underground three seconds in. You reemerge from the ground after 10 seconds and ascend into the heavens. What is an equation that compares your height relative to sea level, (above ground under ground) where sea level is $y=0$, to the time the roller coaster took?

$Answer$

$(x-1)(x-3)(x-10)^3$
or
$(x-1)(x-3)(x-10)$
